Kill this assumption

Asking ≠ Failing

Many developers think asking is an admission. That it reveals something. That it undermines credibility.

In reality — it means you are doing the job well.

Software is complex. Deadlines are real. No one knows everything at once.

The developers who pretend otherwise are burning hours they could be learning — and burning goodwill they'll need later.

Reveal point by point. Let "doing the job well" land before moving on. This reframe is the emotional core of the whole talk. If they don't believe this, nothing else sticks.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Bigger than you

A team where people ask freely ships faster and builds more trust than one where everyone suffers in silence.

How you ask shapes
team norms —

This is the team argument. Not just "be better for yourself" — asking well is a leadership behavior that changes the culture around you. That's what makes this a career-defining skill.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
The safety layer — this is not a soft-skills detour

Psychological
Safety

The belief that you won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up, asking questions, or admitting mistakes.

Amy Edmondson named it. Google's Project Aristotle proved it: studying 180 teams, psych safety was the single biggest predictor of performance — above individual talent, seniority, or process.

This is an engineering velocity concept. Not an HR concept. A team that asks well catches bugs earlier, ships fewer assumptions, and onboards faster.

Don't rush this slide. The research anchor matters — "Google studied 180 teams" lands differently than "it's important to feel safe." Give the data its moment. Then move to the next slide to make it concrete.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Recognizing it — and what to do

When It's
Missing vs. Present

Signs it's absent
  • Questions get dismissed or redirected
  • Post-mortems blame people, not systems
  • Junior devs stop raising concerns
  • Knowledge stays siloed
Signs it's present
  • Blameless post-mortems — actually blameless
  • "I don't know" from senior devs is normal
  • Questions in public channels get real answers
  • Mistakes surface early, not at launch

If your environment is unsafe — that is a system failure, not a personal one. Name it. And know: your asking habits will compound once you're in a team where it's safe to use them.

The two-column gives people a diagnostic tool. They can immediately place themselves. Don't moralize — just name it plainly. Some people in this room are in unsafe environments right now. The callout gives them permission to not internalize it as a personal failure. That matters.

Rubber duck it first.

Just the sentence. Let it sit. Some people know this technique. Some don't. Either way — the simplicity of the advice deserves space before explanation.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
A real technique with a real name

The Rubber
Duck Technique

Explain your problem out loud — to a duck, a blank doc, a voice memo, an AI. Anything that forces articulation.

More often than not: you find the answer mid-explanation.

When you don't — your explanation becomes your question. The act of writing it is part of solving it.

This is both practical and psychological. Externalizing the problem forces structure. That structure is what makes a question answerable.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
2026 first-line stack

AI as First-Line
Resource

Use AI for
  • Decoding error messages
  • Unfamiliar APIs and patterns
  • Getting a starting point
  • Rubber duck at scale
Escalate to a human when
  • AI's fix didn't work
  • Context-specific to your repo
  • Judgment call required
  • Stakes too high to guess
This is not about replacing mentorship. It's about respecting your team's time. A 2-minute AI conversation that gets you 80% of the way there is a gift to your teammate.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Before any ask — search

This Isn't Optional

  • Paste the exact error into Google
  • Check official docs + GitHub issues for the library
  • Stack Overflow — yes, still
  • Claude / Copilot / ChatGPT for explanation + first attempt

Showing you searched makes your question significantly stronger. It's a signal of respect.

The minimum bar before asking a human: 5-10 minutes of independent investigation. Not to prove yourself — to respect their time. And because you might find the answer yourself.

YEARS 0–3:
STRUCTURE

~8 minutes
The 4-Question framework for this section: What is asking with structure? How do we do it wrong? How do we do it good? How do we do it better?
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Year 0–1 · What is asking with structure?

A Strong Question
Includes

  1. What are you trying to do?
  2. What happened instead?
  3. What have you already tried?
  4. What do you think is wrong?
  5. What kind of help do you need right now?
Reveal one at a time. Pause after #3 — "what have you already tried" is where most junior devs fail. They haven't tried anything. Or they've tried things but can't articulate what.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
How we do it wrong vs. how we do it good

❌ vs ✅

Weak

"My app is broken."

Strong

"Getting a 422 on form submit in my Vue app. Payload looks correct — confirmed in the network tab. Tried validating each field manually. Can't tell which one the server rejects. Can you help me narrow it down?"

Show the weak version first. Pause. Ask "Who has sent this exact message?" — uncomfortable laughter. Then reveal the strong version. The contrast does the teaching.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Year 1–3 · How we do it better

Ask Earlier,
More Precisely

"I think the bug is in the state update — the component renders correctly before the async call returns. Moved the update into a separate function. Same result. Does this look like a timing problem?"

Shows reasoning, not frustration. Invites collaboration. Doesn't hand off the problem completely.

The upgrade from year 0-1 is naming the hypothesis. Even if wrong — "I think it might be X" gives the other person a direction to confirm or redirect. You learn faster either way.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Habits that compound — Years 0–3

The Practical Habits

Name your hypothesis

"I think it might be X" gives direction. Confirm or redirect — you learn either way.

Async channels first

Slack over interrupting focus. Write it like you'd say it in person, then send.

Share a repro

StackBlitz, CodeSandbox, Gist. The smallest version that still breaks.

Close the loop

"Turned out it was a race condition in the effect cleanup." Always follow up.

You don't have to reveal these one at a time — let the audience read. Spend 60 seconds walking through the highlights. The "close the loop" habit is the most undervalued one.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
4-Question check · Years 0–3

How Are We Doing?

What is it?

Getting specific enough that someone else can help without asking 3 clarifying questions first.

How do we do it wrong?

"My app is broken." Too early. Too vague. No preparation. No hypothesis.

How do we do it good?

5-part structure. Named hypothesis. Context on what was tried. Clear ask.

How do we do it better?

Async first. Minimal repro. Close the loop. Route to the right person for this specific problem.

This is the 4-question loop — your framework. It applies to every section. Walk through each quadrant in 30 seconds. This gives the audience a mental model they can return to.

YEARS 3–5:
RESPECT TIME

~6 minutes
The trap at this stage: the illusion of independence. Grinding for 4 hours on something a 5-minute conversation could crack is not independence. It's expensive.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter

Independence ≠
Suffering alone.

Let this sit. This is the reframe for years 3-5. Independence means managing your time well — including knowing when to ask.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Year 3–5 · The blocker ask

Protect Team
Momentum

"I've spent about two hours tracing this deployment failure. Ruled out the config file, env vars, confirmed the local build passes. I think it's the CI pipeline, but I need a second set of eyes on it."

Shows effort. Narrows the problem. Helps the other person jump in at the right level. Doesn't block the team.

This example works because it shows what was tried, what was ruled out, and what's suspected. The other person doesn't have to start from zero. That's the gift of a well-formed ask.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Managing time + blockers — Years 3–5

The Practical Habits

Set a time limit

30–45 min of spinning = time to ask. Thrashing for hours is not independence. It's waste.

Bring a proposed solution

"I'm thinking X — does that seem right?" moves faster than "I don't know what to do."

Know when to escalate

"This might need deeper DB layer knowledge. I've gotten as far as X." Name the gap.

Async before screenshare

Screenshare is a premium ask of someone's focus. Try a well-written message first.

The screenshare point gets nods. Screensharing is expensive — it pulls someone out of their context entirely. Default to async. Escalate only when text genuinely can't convey it.

4-Question check · Years 3–5

How Are We Doing?

What is it?

Managing your investigation time so you ask at the right moment — not too early, not too late.

How do we do it wrong?

Grinding 4 hours solo, then asking defeated. Or asking in 10 minutes with zero investigation.

How do we do it good?

YEARS 5–10:
OWN THE OUTCOME

~6 minutes
At this stage, asking for help shifts from personal uncertainty to decision-making. The questions you ask reveal how you think — not just what you don't know.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter

A well-framed ask
is a leadership behavior.

This is the mindset shift for years 5-10. Asking isn't just about getting help. It's about how you communicate expertise, ownership, and decision-making capability.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Year 5–10 · The tradeoff ask

Ask About
Decisions

"I've narrowed this to two options. Option A is faster but adds technical debt. Option B fits the architecture better but takes longer. I'd like your perspective on which tradeoff makes sense for this release."

Shows ownership. Frames the problem. Invites help making a decision — not a rescue.

The key upgrade from years 3-5: the ask is no longer "help me solve this problem." It's "help me make this decision." That's a fundamentally different relationship with expertise.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Asking as leadership — Years 5–10

The Practical Habits

Bring the right context

Manager and principal may not know your feature. Brief them on why the decision matters.

Know when to decide yourself

Seeking genuine input vs. seeking validation — be honest with yourself about which it is.

Ask in public

Team channels normalize the behavior. Senior devs model the culture they want to see.

Write it down

RFC / short doc > one-off question. Creates a record. Scales the ask across the team.

The "write it down" tip is the highest leverage one at this level. A well-written doc with "here's what I'm thinking, here's what I'm unsure about" is a complete decision artifact.

4-Question check · Years 5–10

How Are We Doing?

What is it?

Asking in ways that advance decisions, not just surface problems. Framing that shows ownership.

How do we do it wrong?

Seeking validation instead of input. Not adapting the frame for the audience. One-off questions with no written record.

How do we do it good?

Tradeoff framing. Context-setting for the audience. Asking in public to normalize it.

How do we do it better?

Write the RFC first. Know when you should just make the call. Model the culture you want around you.

The 4-question summary for year 5-10. The "model the culture" point is the most important — at this stage, your behavior shapes the team's behavior.

06
Section 06

ASYNC-FIRST
COMMUNICATION

~4 minutes
Most development teams in 2026 run across time zones, remote setups, and deep-focus schedules. Async-first is not just a preference — it's a professional skill.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter

Live time is a
premium resource.
Treat it like one.

Big statement. Let it sit. Then explain: most teams are distributed. Synchronous time is the most expensive coordination tool you have. Default to async.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
The default posture

Async-First
Principles

  • Write questions specific enough that no clarification is needed before answering
  • Respect that the answer may come in 2 hours, not 2 minutes
  • Async creates a written record — someone will find it later
  • Documenting the answer is part of the ask

Asking well + documenting answers = the same culture.

The documentation point is underrated. If the answer to your question will matter in 3 months — write it down somewhere findable. Notion, GitHub discussion, team wiki. That's asking at scale.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Underrated tool

Loom Is a Valid Ask

A 2-minute screen recording that shows the bug, explains what you've tried, and asks one specific question can outperform a long Slack message.

Still async. Lower friction. Higher clarity. Creates a record.

Especially useful for visual bugs or flow-level issues that are genuinely hard to describe in text. Add it to your toolkit.

Loom gets overlooked because it feels like more work. It's not — 2 minutes of recording often replaces a 20-minute screenshare. Show, don't just tell.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
When to break the rule

When Sync IS
the Right Call

  • Production is down
  • A hard deadline is imminent
  • Problem is too nuanced to describe without losing critical context
  • Multiple people need the same context simultaneously

Everything else: async first. Always try the message before the meeting.

These are genuinely the right times for sync. Outside these — try the message first. The message might work. And if it doesn't, you've already framed the problem before the call.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Works at every level · every year

The Formula

  1. What am I trying to do?
  2. What exactly is blocking me?
  3. What have I already tried?
  4. What do I think is happening?
  5. What kind of help do I need?
  6. What will I do next if this doesn't work?

Step 6 is underrated. It signals maturity and keeps the conversation moving even if the help doesn't fully land.

Reveal one at a time. Slow down on step 6. "What will I do next" — most people never include this. It transforms the ask from helpless to collaborative. It shows you're engaged in solving, not just handing off.
@Nerajno · #AskBetter
Do stuff… sorry… just don't do this

Common Mistakes

  • Asking too early. No prep forces the other person to start from zero.
  • Waiting too long. 3 hours of thrashing ≠ independence. It's expensive.
  • Being vague. "It doesn't work" is not a question.
  • The handoff ask. "Please fix this" vs. "here's where I'm stuck."
  • Not closing the loop. Always. Without exception.
Run through these quickly — they're all things the audience has done. Tone is dry and self-aware, not preachy. The "handoff ask" is where you can add a story from your own experience.
